Don't Say Good Luck Finds the Laughs Backstage While Life Gets Heavy at Home

Don't Say Good Luck (2026)

Content advisory: A parent's serious illness shown frankly, including sickness and fainting; grief, teen drinking, some strong language and sexual references.

Premise

Sixteen-year-old Sophie Birenbaum (Sunny Sandler) is chasing the lead in her high school’s production of Waitress just as her mother, Elizabeth (Melanie Lynskey), learns her cancer is back. Her parents want to keep the news quiet, so Sophie ends up juggling rehearsals, a crush on her scene partner and a family trying very hard to act normal.

The Take

This could have been a disease-of-the-week weepie. Instead, director Julia Hart and co-writer Laura Hankin, who drew on her own high school years, make a theater-kid movie that happens to have something devastating at its center. It’s about living alongside bad news rather than counting down to it, and that restraint is its smartest choice. The title nods to the stage superstition that wishing an actor “good luck” jinxes the show, and the whole film runs on that same nervous mix of hope and dread.

What Works

Lynskey is the anchor, warm and wry with a steel backbone, and her scenes with Sandler feel lived-in rather than staged: a mom coaching her daughter’s Southern accent for the role tells you everything about them. Hart keeps the score soft and the reactions small, so the big moments hit harder when they come. The theater kids get real affection, from cast warm-up games to a castmate fresh from theater camp who lectures everyone on craft. Bebe Neuwirth makes a combative grandmother read as grief in disguise, while Steve Buscemi and Jon Lovitz supply easygoing laughs.

What May Divide Viewers

At 95 minutes, the film leaves little room for anyone outside the mother-daughter story, so the wider family, including Sophie’s younger twin brothers, gets less time than it deserves, and a couple of the generational clashes play broader than everything around them. Sandler carries most of the emotional weight, and not every beat lands. And Adam Sandler’s Happy Madison name in the credits may set you up for a broad comedy that this isn’t.

Who Should Watch

Current and former theater kids, families with teens looking for something honest to watch together, and fans of small coming-of-age films like Lady Bird. If someone in your life is sick right now, it may feel like a comfort or like too much; you’ll know which.

Your take

Sophie's mom wants to keep her illness quiet for as long as she can. Is that protecting her family, or only postponing the hard part?
